# Daily AI Briefing — November 28, 2026

## AI SAFETY & ALIGNMENT

**A study of backdoor detection in fine-tuned models delivers a result this briefing has seen repeatedly in defense research: no single detector survives both of the adversary's evasion strategies, so the detection stack has to be heterogeneous by design (arXiv:2611.34758, November 25).** The paper builds a common scoring system based on token readouts to compare weight-, activation- and output-based model diffing methods for finding backdoors introduced during fine-tuning, and improves weight- and activation-based methods by incorporating the J-lens and its refinement, the J++-lens. The strongest single method, weight difference decoded through the J++-lens of the base model, ranks a token naming the backdoor among its first ten tokens in 11 of 12 settings. The instructive part is the paired failure modes: when the backdoored model is penalized during fine-tuning for giving the backdoor's words probability on clean inputs, output- and activation-based methods such as ADL and Diff Mining easily break, while weight-based methods are unaffected; conversely, permuting hidden units or training only the layers that read the residual stream hides the backdoor from the weight-based methods but not from ADL and Diff Mining. The scope is paired backdoored-versus-base model comparisons, and the metric is token-level ranking rather than end-to-end incident detection, so treat this as a structured comparison of detection families rather than a validated production pipeline. The operational reading is that weight-space and behavior-space detectors fail under complementary pressure, and a defender relying on one family has a blind spot the other family covers.

## AI EVALUATION

**The day's most consequential paper is an evaluation-validity result that lands on this briefing's long-running red-teaming thread: the measured false negative rate of an AI safety monitor is a property of the attack policy used to test it, and that choice can move the number by more than an order of magnitude (arXiv:2611.34934, November 25).** AI monitors, classifiers that flag dangerous behavior by AI agents, are commonly evaluated against transcripts generated by attack policies acting as proxies for dangerous deployment behavior. The authors evaluate an ex-production OpenAI monitor as a case study and find the measured false negative rate ranged from 0.5 to 20.8 percent across optimization levels of a single method, reinforcement learning, and from 6.4 to 23.0 percent across methods at their best observed optimization level, comparing optimized prompting against transcript editing. Every attack policy was developed against proxy monitors only, and all reported results show attacks transferring to a held-out GPT-5.4 target monitor, which strengthens the finding considerably. Two further points carry the weight. The proxy transcripts introduce visible artifacts, such as reasoning about a side task absent from the instructions shown to the monitor, that affect monitor judgments, and the authors state that interpreting any particular result as evidence about deployment requires a separate legitimacy argument for why the proxies are informative, which they leave as an open problem. The stated conclusion is direct: an evaluation using only basic attack methods may give false confidence that a monitor is strong. For anyone running or reading monitor evaluations, the actionable point is that the attack-generation method is a hidden degree of freedom large enough to change the conclusion, and it should be reported and justified, not defaulted.

**A controlled evaluation of influence functions provides the first systematic test, rather than an assumption, of whether the training examples these methods rank as influential actually bear the properties relevant to a model's output (arXiv:2611.34373, November 25).** The authors evaluate three prominent Hessian approximation methods, GradSim, TrackStar and K-FAC, in two settings, linguistic phenomena and factual knowledge, on GPT-2 type models trained on up to 5.6 billion tokens, using re-training with controlled data splits as ground truth. The finding is mixed rather than negative: on average the influential examples are indeed relevant, but they do not always bear the target properties directly, and the ratio and kind of relevance vary by setting. The scale caveat is material: this is the small-model regime, where retraining is affordable, so the result warrants verification at modern scales before generalizing. Influence functions are increasingly proposed as a tool for attribution claims in audits and data-provenance disputes; this paper's message is that those claims currently rest on methods whose validity was assumed rather than tested, and the first test supports only a qualified version of the assumption.

**A reward-integrity study for text-to-SQL reinforcement learning quantifies a hack and then demonstrates, in passing, why single-seed training comparisons should be distrusted (arXiv:2611.34363, November 25).** Standard RL for text-to-SQL pays a reward when the agent's query result matches a reference on one database, but a wrong query can match by luck when the data lack the rows that would expose its bug. The proposed counterfactual-database reward also runs the final query on a few hidden, code-generated copies of the database and pays only if all results match, the strict form, or by the share matched, the mean form. On 1,302 synthetic audit questions, the strict form lowered the share of buggy queries paid in full from 29.5 to 16.8 percent and the share of reward hacks from 54.1 to 1.9 percent, while rejecting none of 2,847 correct queries. The honest part follows: in a controlled GRPO comparison on a 3.35 billion parameter model with three seeds per arm, training on the mean form did not improve in-domain accuracy, because the two rewards disagreed on only 5 to 7 percent of rollouts, and the effect on the Spider benchmark was 1.6 points, with one seed reversing the sign of both comparisons. The transferable finding is the statistical one: reward differences of this size are inside seed noise, and any single-seed ablation of a reward function is uninterpretable.

**A contamination-conscious benchmark of human-written puzzles offers the public counterpart to the private-benchmark approach this briefing described on November 27, and its design choices are the reportable content (arXiv:2611.34331, November 25).** Puzzling-Bench comprises 23,777 puzzles with community-verified answers drawn from the Puzzling Stack Exchange archive from 2014 to 2026, spanning riddles, ciphers, logic grids, lateral-thinking problems, wordplay and recreational mathematics, a third of them with images. Every puzzle carries the author-accepted answer, a reference answer checked against a 50-puzzle human review, the author's own hints, community tags and a creation date, which supports hint ablations, category analysis and a rolling post-cutoff slice as the contamination check: 254 puzzles were posted after the training cutoff of six of the nine models evaluated, including the best one. Results: GPT-6.1 leads at 74.8 percent on a shared 255-puzzle set; a hard core of 17 puzzles defeats all nine models; model failures are strongly correlated, yet an oracle over all nine reaches 91 percent, so the failures are not redundant. The scoring is a two-tier system, normalized exact match then an LLM judge, and the judge validation is unusually careful: three judges from two model families agree on 95 to 97 percent of verdicts, agree with each other at kappa 0.82 to 0.96, move no model by more than 1.6 points, and show no own-family preference. The trade-off against the private-problems approach is explicit: everything is public and per-row provenance is released, so auditing is possible, but the contamination defense shifts to post-cutoff slicing, a smaller and non-random subsample. On the multimodal pilot, withholding a question's image cost the two best models 24 and 35 points.

## AI GUARDRAILS

**A new attack paper shows that media authenticity is no longer the load-bearing assumption in content defenses: coordinated posts made entirely of authentic images and locally faithful captions can steer vision-language agents to attacker-chosen conclusions through the agents' own reasoning (arXiv:2611.34603, November 25).** The authors call the threat visual cognitive collusion and introduce a benchmark of 544 target cases with 33,200 candidate images across eight application domains, plus a multi-agent attack framework that handles narrative planning, image selection and ordering, and caption assignment under iterative review. Across 12 vision-language models, using identical attack feeds with no victim-specific optimization, mean attack success rates were 61.93 percent for proprietary models and 68.71 percent for open-weight models, and 92.65 percent of generated feeds passed an independent model-based validity audit. After downstream review, residual attack success ranged from 35.3 to 76.7 percent across evaluated configurations, so post-hoc review reduces but does not remove the threat. A counterintuitive ablation: adding captions improves model-rated posting naturalness while reducing attack success, suggesting the most deceptive configurations need not look least natural. The caveats are standard for this genre: the benchmark and the attack framework come from the same authors, and the downstream defenses tested are their selected configurations. The threat model matters regardless, because every individual element is authentic; no manipulated media is required, and provenance checks on images or text would not flag it. The paper motivates safeguards that assess how authentic posts jointly shape an agent's reasoning, which is a different detection target than content authenticity.

**A staged fairness audit of a production-scale moderation pipeline localizes where subgroup asymmetry can actually be detected, and finds the standard audit location is where the evidence is thinnest (arXiv:2611.34491, November 25).** The subject is a published TikTok classifier that fuses video, speech and on-screen text into one visibility decision for every language community on the platform, typically judged, if at all, by a single accuracy score. The audit holds three stages to one significance standard using only the model's logged decisions, labels and pre-fusion features, with source language as the subgroup axis: what the classifier may read, what its branches encode, and what it decides. The asymmetry is significant at the input-admission stage, where a language-blind cap admits less of a Vietnamese clip than an English one for reasons of segmentation rather than verbosity, and again in what the text branch encodes. It is undetectable at the decision stage, where the smallest subgroup leaves twelve items to test. The methodological conclusion generalizes: an audit reading only final decisions looks where its evidence is sparsest, and the same test staged upstream recovers a disparity the decision-level view returns as noise. This pairs with the November 26 finding on refusal-filtering in bias audits as a second instance of the same principle: what an evaluation excludes or abstracts away is frequently where the disparity lives.

**A repair study addresses a failure mode of deployment economics: knowledge distillation can introduce regressions in trustworthiness even when downstream performance is preserved, and the usual fixes have structural drawbacks (arXiv:2611.34831, November 25).** The original distillation corpus is often unavailable at repair time, and direct preference optimization may alter capabilities inherited from the teacher. The proposed framework, built on the proximal Bregman response function, estimates the required curvature from a surrogate proxy dataset, removing dependence on the original transfer corpus, and extends to preference supervision through an odds-ratio objective that uses preferred and dispreferred response pairs while constraining changes to the distilled model. Reported results: targeted trustworthiness behaviors improve by up to 8 percent across distilled models with less than 1 percent perplexity decrement, positioned as an alternative to re-distillation or unconstrained preference optimization. Moderate confidence is warranted here: the effect sizes are modest, the improvements are self-reported against the authors' own trustworthiness measurements, and the abstract does not specify the threat models covered by "targeted trustworthiness behaviors," so the claim warrants reading the full paper before procurement decisions rest on it.

## GLOBAL & GEOPOLITICAL AI

**A study of whether agent self-evolution transfers across languages extends the multilingual-deployment concern from static probability calibration, covered here on November 26, to the learned improvement loop itself, and finds that self-improvement does not close the language gap (arXiv:2611.34303, November 25).** Because the tasks LLM agents learn from and are evaluated on are almost always in English, the authors built IRTIQA, a benchmark of aligned English, Urdu-translated, and Urdu code-switched variants of DROP, SNIPS, AppWorld and SkillsBench, constructed with a matrix-language code-switching pipeline and human verification. They evaluate three self-evolving frameworks against their unevolved backbones: Tool-R0, self-play reinforcement learning; AgentEvolver, experience-guided reinforcement learning; and Autogenesis, protocol-level evolution. Self-evolution raises capability: AgentEvolver lifts AppWorld goal completion from near zero to 20 to 36 percent across all conditions, and Autogenesis improves SkillsBench pass rate from 16.48 to 22.22 percent. But it rarely closes the language gap. Evolved systems remain stronger in English, by up to 0.26 DROP F1, the gap is largest on multi-step tasks, and code-switching behaves as a distinct condition rather than as degraded translation. On a shared Gemma-4 31B backbone the gap exists before evolution, and reinforcement-learning-based evolution can hurt Urdu reasoning more than English, meaning the improvement loop can actively widen disparity in the language a deployment most needs. The authors' conclusion is the policy-relevant one: language parity must be an explicit design target, because it does not emerge from self-improvement alone. For sovereign-AI programs betting on agentic self-improvement in non-English markets, this is a direct caution.

## TECHNICAL TRENDS

**A deception-infrastructure paper reframes cyber defense around the economics this week's agentic-attack coverage made salient: if LLMs have collapsed the attacker's cost to scan and adapt, the defender's objective should be burning adversary effort, not realistic-looking decoys (arXiv:2611.34770, November 25).** The authors argue that cyber deception should be judged by the cost exchange it imposes rather than by realism or detection, and that the primitive making the exchange favorable is adversary-specific persistent state: each recognized adversary re-enters the world it believes it has compromised, kept coherent with its own past actions and isolated from every other adversary, rather than a honeypot that resets or merges sessions. One architectural choice deserves emphasis for anyone building agent-facing systems: the language model acts only as a constrained renderer of explicit state, never as its store, which keeps the LLM out of the trust path for state consistency. The paper formalizes the trade-off as a Deception Exchange Rate relating mission-weighted adversary effort burned to defender resources spent, and instantiates it in an SSH honeypot pairing Cowrie with a local LLM. Across five open-weight models, deterministic-layer and prompt optimizations cut per-session defender cost by around 63 percent against a naive all-LLM decoy. The scope limits are stated by the authors themselves: this run covers only defender-side evaluation, and both per-adversary state and the adversary side of the exchange rate are specified but not yet measured, so the central economic claim remains untested against a live adaptive adversary. The direction is nonetheless a sensible response to the documented shift in intrusion economics, and it treats compute spend as a strategic quantity on both sides rather than a fixed cost.
