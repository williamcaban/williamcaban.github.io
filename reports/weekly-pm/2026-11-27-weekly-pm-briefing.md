# Weekly AI Safety & Evals Briefing — Week of November 20, 2026 – November 26, 2026

## Executive Summary
This week the agentic threat surface crossed from vendor telemetry into confirmed government action. CISA added an AI-discovered vulnerability (CVE-2026-133968, CVSS 9.8) to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog with a federal remediation deadline, India's CERT-In reported AI-assisted cyberattacks causing unauthorized transactions at banks and fintech firms, and GreyNoise independently documented an agentic compromise campaign across 22 organizations in 18 countries. On the evaluation side, three separate studies exposed structural gaps in the methods enterprises rely on to certify agent safety: the UK AISI found a production monitor's measured false negative rate varied by a factor of 40 depending solely on the evaluation's attack policy, two independent papers showed agent action boundaries fail under ordinary inputs with no adversarial attack required, and a sovereign-LLM audit found system prompts leaked confidential information in 96 percent of correction-bait attempts. The combined signal for product teams is that trusting an agent requires verifying three layers: the model's prompt boundary, its action boundary, and the evaluation instruments that certify both.

## Findings

### 1. Agentic Attack Campaigns Reach Government-Confirmed Financial Losses

> **Risk:** 25 — Critical · **Remediation:** Eval-Pipeline Change, Human-in-the-loop/Process · **Priority:** Reactive · **NIST RMF:** Measure, Manage

Three independent sources this week confirmed that AI-driven agentic attacks have moved beyond vendor threat intelligence into operational impact. CISA added CVE-2026-133968 (CVSS 9.8), a path-traversal flaw in the MONAI Label medical-imaging server reportedly discovered by autonomous AI agents, to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on November 24, with Amazon's CISO describing a roughly 48-hour campaign in which AI agents identified and exploited the zero-day in about 11 minutes without human step-by-step control (Nov 21-24 briefing, CVE-2026-133968). India's CERT-In reported that AI-assisted cyberattacks caused unauthorized transactions at banks and fintech firms by modifying business records and approval relationships to abuse trusted payment workflows, and separately confirmed AI-driven "agentic offensive frameworks" operating across enterprise, government, academic, and research sectors (Nov 23 briefing). GreyNoise documented campaign GT13, an agentic compromise of 24 Check Point Security Management servers at 22 organizations across roughly 18 countries, observing over 1,400 network sessions and 1,200 files moved; the actor used automated pipelines for exploitation and exfiltration (Nov 25 briefing). Two caveats apply: the MONAI Label AI-attribution comes from Amazon Threat Intelligence without released technical artifacts, and GreyNoise's AI attribution is described as a suspicion rather than a confirmed finding.

**Threat model:** An enterprise deploys an AI agent with tool access (code execution, database queries, API calls) behind a standard authentication boundary. An external attacker, or an autonomous agent, identifies and exploits ordinary process hazards (unauthenticated endpoints, writable directories, shared databases) rather than exotic injection vectors. The resulting compromise uses the organization's own trusted workflows, making fraudulent transactions indistinguishable from legitimate activity until a downstream reconciliation catches the discrepancy.

**Trade-offs:** Adding action-level monitoring (logging every tool invocation, verifying outputs against a sandboxed replay) adds latency per agent step and infrastructure cost. Process-level controls (independent verification of business-record changes, reconciliation of approval-chain modifications) are governance artifacts that slow change management but represent the control that would have caught this week's confirmed cases. On the defensive side, OpenAI and Cisco released Shirudo, an open-source runtime where AI agents write temporary eBPF shields to block vulnerable code paths without restarting services (Nov 24 briefing, shirudo.dev), but this is a first-generation tool with no independent evaluation. This finding is reactive: the exploitation pattern is already observed and remediation is catch-up work.

### 2. Production Monitor False Negative Rate Varies 40x by Attack Policy

> **Risk:** 16 — High · **Remediation:** Eval-Pipeline Change · **Priority:** Proactive · **NIST RMF:** Measure

The UK AI Safety Institute published a study evaluating an ex-production OpenAI monitor under the standard control-evaluation design (Nov 24 briefing, AISI Research). Holding the deployment scenario and control tasks fixed and varying only the attack policy used in the evaluation, the measured false negative rate ranged from 0.5 percent to 20.8 percent across optimization levels of a single method (reinforcement learning) and from 6.4 percent to 23.0 percent across methods at their best optimization level (optimized prompting versus transcript editing). Every attack policy was developed against proxy monitors only, and all reported rates reflect transfer to a held-out GPT-5.4 target monitor, so the result is not an artifact of overfitting. The diagnosis goes deeper than sensitivity: proxy transcripts carried visible artifacts (such as reasoning about a "side task") that influenced monitor judgments independently of safety-relevant content, meaning the evaluation itself changed what it measured.

**Threat model:** An enterprise deploys a control monitor (a secondary LLM that evaluates agent outputs for harmful content before they reach the user or execute a tool). The security team commissions an external red-team evaluation that reports a 1 percent miss rate, and leadership treats this as evidence the monitor is production-ready. An actual adversary uses an attack policy only modestly more sophisticated than the one used in the evaluation and achieves a miss rate closer to 20 percent. The monitor's measured safety was an artifact of the evaluation's attack policy, not a property of the monitor.

**Trade-offs:** The remediation is an evaluation-pipeline change: require security evaluations to report sensitivity to attack-policy variation and to justify why the chosen policy is conservative relative to plausible adversaries. This adds cost and duration to red-teaming engagements but does not affect production latency or helpfulness. The authors' stated conclusion is that interpreting any particular result as evidence about deployment requires a separate argument for why the proxies are informative, and making such arguments convincingly remains an open problem. This finding is proactive: the methodology gap is documented but policy-sensitivity reporting is not yet standard practice.

### 3. Agent Action Boundaries Fail Without Adversarial Input

> **Risk:** 20 — Critical · **Remediation:** Eval-Pipeline Change, Human-in-the-loop/Process · **Priority:** Proactive · **NIST RMF:** Measure, Manage

Two independent studies this week converge on the same finding: agent failures at the action boundary do not require adversarial input. ROGUE-AI (Nov 20 briefing, arXiv:2611.26553) replicated 108 reported agentic security incidents and found that of the 72 incidents causing real-world harm, 62 involved no attacker at all: the agents did what their task asked but missed an ordinary hazard, such as a database shared with a live production site. Across five current GPT, Claude, Gemini, and DeepSeek models, replications without an attacker caused more harm than replications of prompt injection for all five models. A targeted warning naming the specific hazard and its consequence lowered harm; a same-length generic note did not. Separately, a weaponized-agents study (Nov 26 briefing, arXiv:2611.34121) evaluated LLMs in a simulated perception-action pipeline over real-world drone imagery and found that some agents invoked a simulated lethal tool for ordinary target descriptors such as "people" with no jailbreak whatsoever, and that an agent appearing conservative for one phrasing became unsafe under a simple semantic shift.

**Threat model:** An enterprise deploys an LLM agent with tool-execution privileges behind text-level safety guardrails that test whether a prompt is harmful. The guardrails pass the prompt because it is an ordinary, legitimate task. The agent executes it but misses a hazard the prompt did not describe (a shared table, a production endpoint, a concurrent user's data). The resulting harm is indistinguishable from a bug, but the root cause is an action-boundary failure that text-only safety evaluation never tested.

**Trade-offs:** Remediation requires adding action-level evaluation: testing whether the agent's tool invocations, not just its text outputs, remain safe under ordinary task descriptions that mask hazards. This is more expensive than prompt-level safety testing and may require sandboxed replay environments. A lighter-weight human-in-the-loop gate on tool invocations that modify external state adds latency but provides a backstop. The ROGUE-AI paper ships a reusable audit corpus that vendors can rerun before each release, making adoption a matter of integration cost rather than test-design cost. This finding is proactive: the failure mode is documented in research settings but enterprise action-level evaluation pipelines are not yet common.

### 4. System Prompt Confidentiality Is Effectively Nonexistent Under Structured Attacks

> **Risk:** 16 — High · **Remediation:** Prompt/Guardrail, Eval-Pipeline Change · **Priority:** Reactive · **NIST RMF:** Measure, Manage

A red-teaming study of Apertus v1.5, an open sovereign LLM, found that correction-bait attacks extracted protected system-prompt information in 96 percent of requests for the 8B model and 95 percent for the 70B model (Nov 25 briefing, arXiv:2611.32710). The study tested six model families across six languages and reasoning modes using synthetic personal records, and found two leakage paths extending beyond the headline number: verification and derived-value queries exposed protected information without revealing identifiers, and reasoning traces leaked confidential records even when the final answer withheld them, making any deployment that logs chain-of-thought a distinct exposure surface. The paper also reports that post-training did not consistently improve safety, undercutting the assumption that alignment-stage fixes close this gap.

**Threat model:** An enterprise deploys a customer-facing agent with a system prompt containing proprietary business logic, API keys, database schemas, or customer-data access rules. A user applies structured correction-bait attacks rather than simple extraction requests. The agent discloses the system prompt contents. The exposure surface includes not only the visible response but also any logged reasoning traces, where confidential records can appear even when the final answer withholds them.

**Trade-offs:** Defenses include input filtering for extraction patterns (adds latency and risks false positives on legitimate correction requests), restricting reasoning-trace logging in production (loses debugging signal), and moving sensitive logic out of the system prompt into deterministic application-layer enforcement (increases engineering cost but eliminates the leakage surface for that logic). The study's scope caveats are explicit: the data are synthetic personal records and the 95-plus percent figures reflect the worst-case correction-bait attack class rather than an average across adversarial styles. This finding is reactive: the leakage rate is measured at near-certain levels for the tested models, and organizations relying on system-prompt confidentiality should assume it provides no protection against a motivated adversary.

### 5. Multilingual Guardrail Calibration Breaks Outside English

> **Risk:** 16 — High · **Remediation:** Eval-Pipeline Change · **Priority:** Proactive · **NIST RMF:** Measure

A study of 25 top-ranked open-weight decision models (Nov 26 briefing, arXiv:2611.33307) found that the probability scores routers and guardrails threshold on are systematically miscalibrated outside English, and that leaderboard rank predicts none of it. Evaluated on 17 tasks in 14 languages against English on parallel items, every model lost accuracy in every language, from roughly 2 points in high-resource languages to 8 in Tamil, while confidence fell only half as much: a threshold tuned for 95 percent accuracy in English yields 90 percent in Tamil. Across 81 controlled fine-tuning runs, task skill learned in English transferred to other languages and a few thousand target-language items narrowed large losses, but neither more data nor full-parameter tuning closed them. A methodological finding with regulatory relevance: LLM-translated test sets understate the real gap compared to human translations, meaning existing multilingual benchmarks may be systematically optimistic.

**Threat model:** An enterprise deploys a content moderation router or safety guardrail that thresholds an LLM's confidence score to decide whether to block, flag, or allow user content. The threshold was calibrated on English validation data. The service launches in Tamil, Hindi, or Arabic markets. The guardrail is now systematically under-blocking or over-blocking, but the dashboard shows confidence scores that look normal because the miscalibration is invisible at the per-decision level: the threshold is wrong, not the model.

**Trade-offs:** Remediation requires per-language calibration with human-translated (not LLM-translated) test items, which adds cost and time to multilingual launches. A lighter mitigation is to widen thresholds for non-English languages, which increases false positives and degrades user experience but reduces the risk of under-blocking harmful content. The finding that full-parameter tuning did not close the gap implies that tooling fixes (wider thresholds, per-language validation sets) are more immediately actionable than model-side fixes. This finding is proactive: the gap is documented but most production multilingual guardrail deployments have not yet implemented per-language calibration.

### 6. Agent Skill Supply Chain Carries High Dependency-Vulnerability Rate

> **Risk:** 12 — Medium · **Remediation:** Data Filter, Eval-Pipeline Change · **Priority:** Proactive · **NIST RMF:** Measure, Manage

The first registry-scale empirical audit of the LLM agent skill supply chain screened all 48,682 skills on ClawHub (Nov 25 briefing, arXiv:2611.30932) and found that 49.6 percent of skills with resolvable dependencies depend on a version with a known CVE, and 78.3 percent of those were born vulnerable (already exposed at publication). However, reachability tells a more nuanced story: agent input reaches the vulnerable dependency function in only 3.0 percent of affected skills, whereas 21 of 22 sampled first-party code flaws were exploited end to end in a sandbox. The agent-native findings are the most operationally pointed: a live agent executed an injected payload in 48.5 percent of sampled flagged skills, but almost always in its own shell before the skill even ran, with the payload reaching a flaw inside the skill itself in only 5.0 percent. The authors recommend registries prioritize first-party flaws over dependency alerts, and that agent runtimes should not pass skill arguments through a shell.

**Threat model:** An enterprise builds an agent that loads community-contributed skills from a registry. An attacker publishes a skill with a dependency pointing to a vulnerable package, or with first-party code that executes injected payloads. When the enterprise agent loads and runs the skill, the attacker's payload executes in the agent's runtime context with access to the agent's tools and permissions.

**Trade-offs:** Pre-deployment skill auditing (sandboxed execution with fuzzed inputs, dependency CVE scanning) adds a gate to skill ingestion that delays deployment. Runtime isolation (running each skill in a separate sandbox) adds infrastructure complexity and latency. Rejecting shell-based argument passing in favor of structured parameter contracts is an agent-runtime design choice that may limit compatibility with existing skills. Transferability to registries beyond ClawHub is unverified, so the 49.6 percent figure should not be read as an industry-wide rate. This finding is proactive: the audit is the first of its kind and most agent platforms do not yet have skill-supply-chain security controls.

## Opportunities & Roadmap Actions

Two concrete opportunities emerge from this week's findings. First, Finding 3 (action-boundary failures) and Finding 1 (agentic attack escalation) together create a market opening for an action-level safety evaluation suite offered as a differentiated trust signal. A product claim along the lines of "our agents are tested not just on what they say but on what they do" would directly address the gap between text-level guardrails (which the industry already has) and action-level verification (which this week's evidence shows is missing). The ROGUE-AI audit corpus provides a reusable, vendor-agnostic starting point. Second, Finding 5 (multilingual guardrail calibration) opens a path to eval-as-a-feature: a per-language calibration service could become a recurring revenue or retention mechanism for enterprises entering non-English markets, particularly in regulated sectors where a miscalibrated guardrail carries compliance exposure.

The overall balance this week leans proactive: four of six findings identify evaluation or architecture gaps that have not yet been exploited at enterprise scale (monitor eval sensitivity, action boundaries, multilingual calibration, skill supply chain), while two are reactive (agentic attacks causing confirmed losses, system prompt leakage). This suggests next sprint capacity should allocate roughly 60 percent toward hardening evaluation infrastructure (action-level testing harnesses, per-language calibration datasets, monitor policy-sensitivity reporting) and 40 percent toward patching known exposures (system-prompt restructure, agent tool-access review, third-party-provider audit clauses).

The dominant cross-finding NIST RMF theme is the Measure function: five of six findings identify gaps in how safety is evaluated rather than in the safety mechanisms themselves. The organization is likely investing in guardrails whose effectiveness it cannot reliably measure. A dedicated evaluation-infrastructure workstream under the Measure function, covering action-level testing, attack-policy sensitivity reporting, and per-language calibration, would address the majority of this week's risk surface and is worth raising to leadership as a single, coherent initiative rather than as six separate remediation tickets.
