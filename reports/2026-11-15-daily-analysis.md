# Daily AI Briefing — November 15, 2026

## AI SAFETY & ALIGNMENT

**A JMLR paper removes two long-standing frictions from safe sequential decision-making, and the removals matter for any system that must learn without violating an unknown constraint.** The paper (Bottero, Luis, Vinogradska, Berkenkamp and Peters, JMLR 27(145), 2026) addresses safe Bayesian optimization, where an unknown function must be optimized through noisy evaluations that may not violate an a-priori unknown safety constraint. The authors observe two structural limitations of existing methods: most discretize the domain and cannot be directly extended to continuous spaces, and they impose regularity assumptions on the safety constraint that introduce an additional critical hyperparameter. Their information-theoretic safe exploration criterion instead exploits only the Gaussian process posterior to pick the safe parameters most informative about the safety of other parameters, combined with a standard Bayesian optimization acquisition function. They prove the resulting criterion can learn about the reachable safe optimum to arbitrary precision while only evaluating safe parameters with high probability, and report data-efficiency and scalability in experiments. The practical relevance extends beyond optimization: any deployed system that explores under a safety constraint it cannot observe directly faces the same two problems, and this line of work treats them as first-class objects rather than nuisance assumptions.

## AI EVALUATION

**For the first time in a while, no OpenAI model tops the MathArena math benchmark: Google's Gemini 4 Carbon now leads, and the cost spread behind that ranking is a cautionary tale about how benchmark wins are framed.** Per The Decoder's November 14 report, Gemini 4 Carbon (high reasoning) leads the overall recommendation table at 85.4 percent plus or minus 3.0, with Anthropic's Claude-Fable-5.2 second at 82.8 plus or minus 5.3 and GPT-6.1 Sol third at 80.6 plus or minus 2.7. These are expected-performance figures with uncertainty intervals rather than point scores, which is the right way to present benchmark results and worth noting in itself. The cost figures are the substance behind the headline: at the tested reasoning settings, Carbon cost roughly 15 times as much as GPT-6.1 Sol (about $15.70 versus $1.07 per evaluation) and Fable 5.2 about 40 times as much. Behavioral detail matters for anyone building eval harnesses: Carbon's most common failure mode was exceeding the time limit, and it almost never gave up on a task, including one case where it launched a script that ran over eleven hours without checking progress. The best open-weight model, Kimi K3.1 Pro, trailed the field at 54.3 percent, so the frontier-to-open gap in mathematics remains wide. These figures come from the benchmark's own published tables as reported by The Decoder; independent replication of the vendor model settings is the standard caveat.

## AI GUARDRAILS

**Cursor's forensic update on the trojanized Claude Code extension incident shows a complete third-party software supply chain failure, and the mechanism deserves more attention than the malware itself.** Per Cursor's Friday update reported by The Hacker News (November 15), the chain ran as follows. An attacker obtained an access key Cursor had issued to Fireworks AI, which was exposed in a breach of Fireworks. The key gave access to a read-only storage bucket Cursor used to share data with Fireworks, containing coding agent traces from sessions not in Privacy Mode, most dating to 2025 and permitted for model training use. Those traces contained credentials that had not been edited out, exposing API credentials of fewer than 0.01 percent of customers. The attacker pivoted from those credentials into Cursor's SaaS tooling, reaching archived source code repositories, limited customer support data, usage metrics, summaries of some agent conversations, and credentials users had sent to the support team. On October 29 the attacker used a stolen internal credential to place a trojanized version of Anthropic's official Claude Code extension on the cache serving Cursor's extension marketplace; at most 5,000 users requesting specific cached versions on that date potentially received the malicious build, which was live for approximately 11 hours before removal and attempted to install a persistent SSH backdoor. Cursor says it deactivated the vendor key, revoked and rotated credentials, and temporarily took internal services offline during containment. The transferable lesson is about data retention and laundering: agent conversation traces collected for training became the pivot point for an infrastructure compromise months later, and credentials that arrive through support channels are part of the attack surface. Vendors that share data with upstream model providers may want to re-read those agreements with this failure mode in mind.

## GLOBAL & GEOPOLITICAL AI

**The Australian AI policy debate has moved from the safety regulator to copyright and property rights, and the opposition is now fracturing along lines that complicate the government's investment agenda.** Updating the November 12 report on Australia's draft frontier AI safety legislation, The Guardian reported November 15 that Liberal senator Andrew McLachlan has urged that artists' copyright not be sacrificed for investment, saying artists' works "must not be sacrificed on the altar of global corporate profiteering" and calling datacentre growth an environmental concern. The legal core is serious rather than rhetorical: several Coalition senators back concerns, raised by the recording industry body Aria, that permitting AI companies to scrape copyrighted material could amount to an acquisition of property by the government, which under the Australian constitution must occur "on just terms," a doctrine legal experts earlier warned could support a large compensation case. Anthropic has put forward an opt-out model that would allow scraping unless a rights holder objects, and AI companies say they need legal certainty on copyright before investing in Australia, while Labor and the opposition leadership have signaled a bipartisan approach. McLachlan's stated condition, that any compensation be considered on an individual basis for each artist rather than through an industry-wide scheme, would make any market or licensing regime substantially harder to design. The practical significance for safety policy: the safety regulator and the copyright framework are being negotiated as one package, so the constitutional challenge has become a potential lever over the entire AI suite, with the package expected before the end of the year.

## TECHNICAL TRENDS

**Runway has released its robotics model Praxis-1 with open weights and source code under Apache 2.0, and the documented limitations are as informative as the benchmark lead.** The model, introduced in late September and now public with a detailed research report, builds on a video model that learned how objects move and interact from ordinary footage, fine-tuned with videos of human actions and robot data. It processes camera images, text instructions and position data to predict how the environment will change and derive robot movements. On Nvidia's RoboLab-120 simulation benchmark, Runway reports a 46.2 percent success rate versus 42.9 for Black Forest Labs' FLUX 3 Action, topping the World Action Model category though ranking fifth overall behind two AI agents and two differently architected robot models; Runway ran all tests itself and the results are not independently verified. Adaptation still needs substantial robot data: roughly 277 hours from the public DROID dataset for a Franka arm and 1,400 to 2,600 hours for a dual-arm robot, so whether video pretraining genuinely reduces total robot-data requirements remains, as the report itself notes, an open question. The limits the company documents are the ones that matter for deployment: the model sometimes confuses similar-looking objects, struggles with vague instructions, and cannot yet reliably plan complex action sequences on its own, which positions it as an execution layer to be paired with a separate task-planning model rather than an autonomous agent. Practical requirements are nontrivial: the standard version needs an 80 GB GPU, with a compressed version estimated but not yet measured to fit a 32 GB RTX 5090.

**A former frontier lab researcher's argument that algorithmic efficiency is improving at an accelerating rate, if correct, changes the calculus behind compute-governance policy, though the evidence is a single leaked chart and the claim should be treated accordingly.** Per The Decoder's November 14 report, Jacob Coxon, formerly of OpenAI and Anthropic, says a chart from Anthropic's IPO roadshow showing training compute requirements over time implies algorithmic efficiency improving not just quickly but superexponentially, and that the company's Mythos model could already be retrained with a fraction of its original compute. His conclusion, a call to pause frontier development next year so competitors can catch up, is an advocacy position and the chart's compressed axis makes visual rate assessment treacherous. No direct citation for the underlying efficiency figures is available outside the roadshow material itself; treat as working assumption pending verification. The reason the argument belongs in a technical briefing rather than an opinion section: export controls, safety-case regimes and compute thresholds all assume compute is the binding input, and any strong algorithmic-efficiency trend erodes that assumption directly.
