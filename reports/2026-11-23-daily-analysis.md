# Daily AI Briefing — November 23, 2026

## AI GUARDRAILS

**India's national cyber response agency has moved the agentic-attack question from threat intelligence into confirmed financial losses: CERT-In reports that AI-assisted cyberattacks caused unauthorized transactions at banks and fintech firms, and, separately, that threat hunting has uncovered AI-driven "agentic offensive frameworks" operating across enterprise, government, academic, and research sectors.** The advisory, issued last week and reported November 23 (The Hacker News), describes a pattern that deserves attention precisely because it is unglamorous: rather than attacking payment APIs directly, attackers targeted business records and approval processes inside otherwise legitimate systems, modified business records or approval relationships, and then used the organization's own trusted payment workflows, so the fraudulent transactions raised no alerts. The named exploitation point is the security blind spot in independent verification, reconciliation, and review of changes to business records, a process-layer control rather than a technical one. The agency disclosed no loss amounts and made no attribution. The threat-hunting findings read as a field confirmation of the escalation this briefing has tracked since the MONAI Label incident of November 21: automated reconnaissance to map exposed systems and attack paths, rapid generation and modification of exploits by analyzing software patches, clusters of AI agents running intrusion tasks such as privilege discovery in parallel, and adaptive modification of scripts and attack methods in response to errors or blocked attempts, all while using valid credentials and legitimate administrative functions to move deeper. CERT-In's guidance mirrors the ROGUE-AI replication finding from the November 20 report that most agentic harm stems from ordinary process hazards rather than exotic injection: review the security of business applications, critical records, user identities, and third-party interfaces, and make sure monitoring and incident response account for misuse of trusted business processes, not only for malicious code. For financial institutions the operational takeaway is that the transaction itself is indistinguishable from legitimate activity once the approval chain is corrupted, so the control that would have caught these cases is change verification, which is a governance artifact rather than a model-safety layer.

## GLOBAL & GEOPOLITICAL AI

**The Indian advisory also carries a geopolitical edge that the security press largely omitted: the deployment of agentic offensive frameworks is no longer inferred from vendor telemetry in Western networks, it is confirmed by a national CERT in a sovereign-AI state, which reframes the regulatory timeline debate that stalled in Geneva last week.** The CERT-In findings list sectors hit, not just techniques, and the advisory's own framing, "unsanctioned activity" causing "unauthorized financial transactions at banking and financial services entities," is the language of an agency documenting operational impact within its jurisdiction rather than hypothesizing about capability. This connects to the multilateral failure reported November 22, where the UN Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons conference closed without agreement on an autonomous-weapons instrument, and to the Red Cross's warning that the regulatory window is closing. The salient asymmetry, consistent across both stories, is that the offensive use of AI agents is self-propagating through criminal and state ecosystems at a pace that the treaty process cannot match, and defensive posture is now being set by national agencies acting alone. India's advisory is the clearest example to date of a regulator telling its own financial and government sectors, in effect, that the AI-agent threat model is current rather than prospective. No direct citation is available for the broader claim that other national CERTs hold comparable unpublished findings; treat as working assumption pending verification.

## TECHNICAL TRENDS

**A new JMLR paper gives stochastic bilevel optimization its first sharp complexity treatment for heavy-tailed noise, and the result is directly relevant to the training regimes where the usual light-tail assumptions quietly fail.** Stochastic Bilevel Optimization with Heavy-Tailed Noise (Liu and Luo, JMLR 27(233), 2026) studies smooth bilevel problems with a strongly convex lower level and possibly nonconvex upper level, under the assumption that gradient evaluations are unbiased but corrupted by heavy-tailed noise, a regime the authors note is prevalent in training large language models and reinforcement learning. Their nested-loop normalized stochastic bilevel approximation, N²SBA, finds an ε-stationary point with stochastic first-order oracle complexity of order κ^((7p−3)/(p−1)) · σ^(p/(p−1)) · ε^(−(4p−2)/(p−1)), where κ is the condition number, σ the noise level, and p in (1, 2] the order of the central moment of the noise. The specialization to nonconvex-strongly-concave minimax problems achieves a slightly better exponent. The key calibration for practitioners: all these upper bounds match the best-known results in the bounded-variance setting, which corresponds to p = 2, meaning the framework covers heavy tails at no asymptotic penalty relative to the classical case when p = 2, and degrades gracefully as p decreases. The honest caveats are standard for this genre: these are worst-case oracle bounds, not empirical claims, and the numerical experiments establish empirical superiority only against the baselines the authors chose. For hyperparameter optimization and other bilevel pipelines where gradient noise is not provably light-tailed, the paper supplies the first cost accounting that matches the classical setting.

**A second JMLR paper formalizes the infrastructure behind a fast-growing research area: torchgfn (Viviano, Younis, Choi, Schmidt, Bengio and Lahlou, JMLR 27(142), 2026) is a PyTorch library for generative flow networks whose core contribution is architectural rather than algorithmic.** GFlowNets sample proportionally to reward rather than concentrate on a mode, and their adoption across communities with very different backgrounds has produced, per the authors, a need for a common substrate where new training losses and policies can be tested against standard benchmark implementations on shared environments. The library decouples environments, neural network modules, and training objectives into interchangeable components behind a single API, with examples that replicate and unify published results. Two points matter beyond the library announcement itself. First, for a field where losses are still an active research frontier (multiple competing training objectives coexist), a decoupled reference implementation is what makes fair loss comparison reproducible, which is an evaluation-infrastructure contribution as much as a software one. Second, the availability on GitHub and PyPI makes the standard benchmarks themselves a moving target; claims of superiority over "standard implementations" should now be expected to cite a specific torchgfn version, since the decoupled design invites rapid churn in both losses and environments.
