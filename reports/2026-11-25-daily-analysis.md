# Daily AI Briefing — November 25, 2026

## AI SAFETY & ALIGNMENT

**An Axios report published November 24, citing more than two dozen insiders at OpenAI and Anthropic, states that many employees at both companies now consider their unreleased frontier models too capable to be safely shipped, and that internal debates about halting releases, or even stopping training, are underway.** The Decoder's coverage of the report adds that employee trust in leadership is described as eroding. The claims come from anonymous insiders and neither company has published corroborating technical evidence, so the appropriate reading is a signal about internal governance strain rather than a verified capability statement. It nonetheless lands one day after OpenAI's own proposal to use perplexity as a shared coordination metric for frontier pace, and together the two items describe the same problem from opposite ends: labs are looking for externally measurable yardsticks precisely because internal oversight confidence is faltering. Neither report establishes what the specific failure modes are, and no independent evaluation of the unreleased systems exists. No direct citation is available for the models' specific capabilities; treat the Axios account as attributed insider testimony pending verification.

**The agentic attack thread that has run through this briefing since November 21 now has a third, independent data point, and this one comes from network telemetry rather than from a vendor or a national CERT: GreyNoise has documented a campaign, tracked as GreyNoise Threat 13 (GT13), that compromised 24 Check Point Security Management servers belonging to 22 organizations across roughly 18 countries, with attack activity confirmed between October 17 and November 2, 2026.** GreyNoise assesses, as a suspicion rather than a confirmed finding, that the actor used AI-driven "agentic harnesses" for vulnerability scanning, exploitation, and automated interaction with compromised assets, observing 99 IP addresses (mostly disposable infrastructure or victim devices), over 1,400 network sessions, 800-plus command-line artifacts, and 1,200 files moved. In one documented chain, an automated pipeline exploited a critical Rejetto HFS flaw (CVE-2026-61500) to obtain a relay server, then exfiltrated data from the pre-compromised Check Point servers; GreyNoise states it could not determine when or how initial access to the Check Point assets was gained. On one victim network the actor ran a script for the already-patched Check Point flaw CVE-2026-16232 to launch over 100 management tasks, stealing firewall databases and certificate authority files, minting certificates, and unloading security policies to disable firewall protections. Two disciplines matter when reading this. First, Check Point's own threat intelligence director stated the exploited flaw was previously disclosed with patches available and represents no new vulnerability or technique, so the novel element is the suspected automation apparatus, not the exploitation. Second, the AI attribution remains GreyNoise's theory; no technical artifact demonstrating agent involvement has been released. What the campaign adds to the MONAI Label and CERT-In cases is breadth and independence: sectors hit include financial services, energy, government, transportation, mining, and manufacturing, across the UK, Europe, India, East Asia, Australia, and the US, and the telemetry comes from a research network rather than a party with a product or jurisdictional stake.

## AI EVALUATION

**A single-author red-teaming study of Apertus v1.5, an open sovereign LLM, provides the sharpest quantified refutation yet of the assumption that system-prompt confidentiality instructions protect sensitive information: Apertus 8B and 70B leaked protected information in 96 and 95 percent of requests, respectively, under correction-bait attacks (arXiv:2611.32710, submitted November 24).** The study red-teams Apertus and five other model families across six languages and reasoning modes, using synthetic personal records and testing direct disclosure, correction baits, yes/no verification, derived-value queries, reasoning leakage, and authorization checks. Two findings extend beyond the headline number. Verification and derived-value queries expose protected information without necessarily revealing identifiers, meaning leaks can occur even when no name is ever printed. And reasoning traces can leak confidential records even when the final answer withholds them, which makes any deployment that logs chain-of-thought a distinct exposure surface. The paper also reports that post-training does not consistently improve safety, a result that matters for sovereign-AI procurement narratives: a state choosing an open model for sensitive workloads cannot rely on alignment-stage fixes or instruction-level controls, and the author argues for multilingual, reasoning-aware safety evaluation as the required baseline. The scope caveats are explicit in the design: the data are synthetic personal records, and leak rates under correction-baiting are the worst-case attack class rather than an average across adversarial styles, so the 95-plus percent figures should be read as a bound on a specific vector, not a general leakage rate.

**A new benchmark for uncertainty quantification in LLM-based recommenders, published in Transactions on Machine Learning Research (arXiv:2611.33036, November 24), tackles an evaluation gap most UQ comparisons in this space have simply ignored: LLM rankers have no standard classification head, so different UQ methods draw uncertainty from different sources and apply it at different stages, making published comparisons largely incommensurable.** The benchmark fixes the ranking and evaluates eight heterogeneous UQ signals as record-level monitors under a shared output contract, across four recommendation datasets and two LLM backbones. The three results are instructive for anyone trusting uncertainty numbers from an LLM pipeline. Method ordering changes across backbones even on the same reference instances, so a UQ method validated on one model does not transfer claims to another. A ranking-aligned signal (Top10Mass) gives the strongest scalar error detection at the top-10 cutoff in several conditions, but its advantage narrows for popularity-tail targets, exactly where recommendation failures concentrate. And scalar UQ signals are weak at identifying which of two controlled evidence sources produced the larger NDCG@10 difference, whereas source-specific signals such as perturbation rank instability do better, implying that a single uncertainty scalar is the wrong abstraction for diagnosing ranker failures. This is an infrastructure contribution in the same spirit as this week's Apertus study: it makes comparison possible where previously only incompatible numbers existed.

## AI GUARDRAILS

**A generative attack framework called SALSA changes the economics of adversarial-suffix evaluation from per-prompt optimization to one-time training followed by cheap sampling (arXiv:2611.33030, November 24).** Instead of running expensive GCG-style discrete optimization against each target, SALSA trains a generative model on a corpus of optimization-derived attack suffixes, then samples suffixes directly. On open-source targets it achieves attack success rates comparable to state-of-the-art optimization baselines while cutting generation time from 3.46 seconds to 0.04 seconds per prompt. In black-box transfer against proprietary systems it reports 99 percent ASR on GPT-3.5-Turbo and 45 percent on GPT-4o, which the authors state is more than double the strongest baseline at an equal sampling budget. The security-relevant point is the shift in cost structure: once a suffix distribution is learned, evaluating a new target's robustness becomes nearly free, which cuts both ways. Defenders get a scalable continuous-assessment primitive for probing their own models, but the same property means jailbreak testing at scale no longer requires meaningful compute per attempt. The honest limitations: the strongest results against modern targets are transfer numbers against two OpenAI endpoints, the intuition offered for why sampling preserves success (the learned distribution staying close to the optimization output distribution) is presented as an argument rather than a proof, and results are dated to the models named.

**A statistics paper attacks a hole in watermark provenance practice: reviewers do not evaluate whole documents, they select passages, and existing watermark confidence statements do not survive that selection process (arXiv:2611.32674, November 24).** The paper develops simultaneous lower confidence bounds on the fraction of selected tokens that retain a watermark signal, valid jointly for the full document and for any passages a reader chooses during review, including selections made after examining watermark evidence, which is the setting where naive confidence statements break. The method, structure-adaptive closed testing (SACT), uses a working model of sequential signal clustering to build scores for calibrated sign-based tests, and the bounds retain finite-sample simultaneous validity under a conditional null even when the working model is misspecified, which is the property that makes the guarantee trustworthy in practice. In simulations across two watermarking mechanisms and a range of signal strengths and densities, SACT generally shows higher power than competing methods, and assessing related passages together yields sharper bounds on their combined signal. This is a modest-seeming but consequential addition to the provenance stack: it moves watermark evidence from document-level detection toward legally and editorially meaningful quantities, namely what fraction of the excerpt under review carries the signal, with stated error control.

**The first registry-scale empirical audit of the LLM agent skill supply chain screened all 48,682 skills on ClawHub and, after manual review of 3,428 flagged cases and sandbox reachability testing with a live LLM agent under indirect prompt injection, produced a result that inverts the industry's current patching priorities (arXiv:2611.30932, November 23).** The study, which covers two carried-over risks (third-party dependencies and first-party code) and two agent-native ones (install instructions and credentials printed to stdout), found that 49.6 percent of skills with resolvable dependencies depend on a version with a known CVE, and, unlike the npm ecosystem where packages mostly become vulnerable after release, 78.3 percent of these were born vulnerable, already exposed at publication. But reachability tells the opposite story: agent input reaches the vulnerable dependency function in only 3.0 percent of affected skills, whereas 21 of 22 sampled first-party flaws were exploited end to end in the sandbox. The agent-native findings are the most operationally pointed: a live agent executed an injected payload in 48.5 percent of sampled flagged skills, but almost always in its own shell before the skill even ran, with the payload reaching a flaw inside the skill itself in only 5.0 percent. The authors' two recommendations follow directly: registries should prioritize first-party flaws over dependency alerts, and agent runtimes should not pass skill arguments through a shell. For a platform ecosystem only now industrializing skill distribution, this is the audit that was needed before, not after, a major incident; the caveat is that ClawHub's population and tooling may not generalize to other registries.

**A formal-methods paper extends relative security from confidentiality to integrity, and the extension exposes a real gap in how prompt-injection defenses are currently evaluated (arXiv:2611.29802, November 22).** Relative integrity states that every behavior a system exhibits under attacker influence must be one the reference system can exhibit for the same trusted inputs. Combining relative security and relative integrity is still insufficient, because each chooses its own reference runs, so together they accept attacks whose information leak and whose effects the reference reproduces only in different runs. The paper therefore defines relative robustness, which requires the same runs to reproduce both, and shows it is strictly stronger. Evaluated on models of five published attacks, a support desk, an OP-TEE HOTP token, and common agent defenses, two cases pass both relative security and relative integrity but fail relative robustness: a support-desk injection that makes the model accept any customer (the Tensor Trust game scenario), and the simulation step of the attack on Secret Network's SGX enclave. Everything is mechanized in Isabelle/HOL with the enclave programs verified in C. The practical takeaway for agent-defense vendors is uncomfortable: a defense can pass both established relative-security checks and still admit a behavior combination no honest reference run would produce, and only the stricter property catches it.

## GLOBAL & GEOPOLITICAL AI

**Forty researchers at TU Darmstadt have published a position paper arguing that computer science should redefine itself as "Computer and AI Science," and the safety-relevant core of the argument is not workforce nostalgia but a claim that a computing paradigm shift is breaking existing verification guarantees.** The paper (available from the TU Darmstadt department site, reported November 24 by The Decoder) identifies three shifts: lower barriers to building software, dramatic research acceleration, and the emergence of what the authors call "Generative Computing," where systems interpret human intent and dynamically generate their own behavior and code at runtime. The sharp question the authors pose is how safety, privacy, and correctness can still be verified and certified when a system's behavior emerges dynamically at runtime rather than being fixed before deployment, with the problem most acute for embodied AI such as robots and autonomous vehicles, where physical actions computed in real time cannot simply be undone. Their prescriptions lean pedagogical: curricula shifting from writing code toward verifying and directing AI-generated systems, mandatory AI-free coursework to preserve fundamentals, and explicit human accountability where safety and speed conflict. The paper also calls for energy efficiency and for the discipline to engage policymakers and civil society rather than leaving AI's trajectory to technological and economic forces alone. It is a position paper, not an empirical study, and the certification-gap claim is a diagnosis rather than a demonstrated failure, but it matters as a formal statement from a major German computer science department at a moment when the EU's AI Act implementation is forcing exactly the conformity-assessment question the paper raises.

**A mechanistic interpretability study accepted to Findings of EACL 2027 (arXiv:2611.32685, November 24) localizes social bias inside multilingual models and finds that where bias lives depends on the language's pretraining footprint, a result with direct implications for how multilingual safety interventions are built.** Across three instruction-tuned models, four languages, and seven bias dimensions, the authors compare two neuron-identification methods and find integrated gap gradients more effective: the neurons it isolates, largely concentrated in the model's deepest layers, produce sharper decreases in bias when suppressed. Two findings carry the weight. Neuron distribution patterns differ between high- and low-resourced languages, suggesting that a language's representation in pretraining data shapes how its biases are encoded internally, which implies that bias mitigation tuned on English data may be aimed at the wrong components for other languages. And neurons linked to gender, nationality, and social class overlap the most with neurons tied to other bias dimensions, marking those three as structural hubs in how models organize bias. The caveats are the usual ones for this genre: suppression-based causal evidence is correlational at the representational level and limited to three models and four languages, so generalization across architectures and the wider language spectrum is unverified.

## TECHNICAL TRENDS

**A JMLR paper reframes digital-twin validation as a causal falsification problem, and its central theorem is a negative result worth internalizing for anyone certifying simulation-based AI systems in safety-critical settings (Cornish, Taufiq, Doucet and Holmes, JMLR 27(152), 2026).** The paper gives a precise causal definition of what it means for a twin to be "correct," then establishes that observational data cannot certify a twin in that sense unless potentially tenuous assumptions, such as unconfoundedness, hold. The authors' response is to invert the goal: rather than trying to prove a twin correct, they present a general-purpose statistical procedure for finding situations in which the twin is demonstrably not correct, a procedure that remains sound even when the data are confounded, requiring only an i.i.d. dataset of observational trajectories. They apply the methodology to a large-scale real-world case study, assessing sepsis modeling within the Pulse Physiology Engine against the MIMIC-III ICU dataset, with code released on GitHub. The broader relevance goes beyond clinical simulation: as model-generated simulations (including learned world models) get proposed as evidence for safety cases, the paper supplies the principled stance that certification-by-observational-agreement is formally unavailable and falsification is the defensible alternative. The limitation is inherent to the approach: passing falsification is evidence of absence of detected flaws in the tested regime, not proof of correctness elsewhere.

**A second JMLR paper gives safe Bayesian optimization a continuous-domain treatment that removes the two practical obstacles that have kept safe BO mostly a discrete-domain technique (Bottero, Luis, Vinogradska, Berkenkamp and Peters, JMLR 27(145), 2026).** The setting is sequential decision making where evaluations must not violate an unknown safety constraint, and both objective and constraint are unknown and modeled with a Gaussian process. Most current methods discretize the domain and cannot extend directly to continuous spaces, and they impose regularity assumptions on the safety constraint that introduce a critical extra hyperparameter. The proposed information-theoretic safe exploration criterion uses only the GP posterior to select, among known-safe parameters, those most informative about the safety of other parameters, combined with a standard BO acquisition function. The theoretical result is that the acquisition function can learn the reachable safe optimum to arbitrary precision while only evaluating safe parameters with high probability, with empirical demonstrations of data efficiency and scalability. The relevance to trustworthy AI deployment is straightforward: tuning a live system (datacenter cooling, dosing, process control) under hard safety constraints needs exploration policies whose guarantees do not depend on hand-tuned hyperparameters, and this supplies one for continuous domains. The standing caveat applies: guarantees are conditional on GP model fidelity, and misspecification of the safety model is exactly the failure the method cannot see.
