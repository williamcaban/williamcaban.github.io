# Daily AI Briefing — November 22, 2026

## AI SAFETY & ALIGNMENT

**Updating the November 21 report on the MONAI Label incident, where attackers' AI agents reportedly discovered the vulnerability themselves: the flaw has now received a formal CVE, and its severity score leaves little ambiguity about where defenders should rank it.** The path-traversal weakness in the open-source medical-imaging server is now published as CVE-2026-133968, rated 9.8 out of 10, critical (The Hacker News, November 22). Two clarifications accompany the assignment. First, the project's own advisory page had not yet listed the CVE at the time of reporting, which the coverage attributes to a lag in updating that page, not to the existence of a second flaw. Second, the earlier cautions stand: the attribution and the claim that the agents found the flaw autonomously come from Amazon Threat Intelligence's published account without released technical artifacts, and the remediation gap noted on November 21, a merged fix with no fixed installable release, is still the operative guidance for the research and hospital networks that run MONAI Label.

## AI EVALUATION

**A JMLR paper moves visual anomaly detection from scoring anomalies to governing the decision itself, and the theoretical target is the gap that most deployed detectors leave open: formal error control under distribution shift.** HiMT, the Hierarchical Martingale-based Testing framework (Wu, Ma, Zou, Gong, Huang and Liu, JMLR 27(232), 2026), starts from a diagnosis the authors make about the field: existing approaches concentrate on designing robust anomaly score functions and largely neglect the decision-making process, which is what actually matters in reliable and safety-critical applications. The framework attacks the two named failure points of that process with different machinery: martingale theory to handle distribution shift, and a hierarchical multiple testing procedure to control decision errors rigorously at both the image level and the pixel level. The construction is worth naming for anyone building evaluation pipelines: rather than a single decision threshold on a score, the hierarchy treats per-image and per-pixel decisions as a family of tests whose error rates are jointly controlled, so guarantees survive the granularity that actual inspection workflows demand. The paper's experimental claim is that the framework verifies superior decision-making capabilities at both levels, with the usual caveat that "superior" is measured against the baselines the authors chose and warrants a read of the full comparisons before adoption.

## AI GUARDRAILS

**The week's most consequential guardrail failure is a supply-chain one: OpenRouter has removed three AI inference providers from its platform after researchers found attackers stealing customer prompts inside those providers for months, with none of the three aware until they were told.** Per the report (The Hacker News, November 22), the industrial cybersecurity company Dragos found the intrusion, and the gateway operator's response went beyond removal: OpenRouter also stripped 125 endpoints from eligibility for Zero Data Retention and notified all potentially affected customers. The mechanism matters as much as the tally. Some of the stolen requests were flagged as Zero Data Retention, the contractual setting that is supposed to mean a prompt is never stored, which is precisely the kind of assurance enterprises buy inference services on. The investigation's forensic anchor was a mismatch between what one provider displayed and what it retained: OpenRouter discloses only the first 8 characters of a provider's request link, and material that surfaced with full links visible indicated whoever posted it was reading a cached copy of the complete prompt, evidence of retention past the disclosed point. The systemic lesson is blunt: a data-retention guarantee is only as strong as the least-audited link in a routing chain, and gateways that pool many third-party providers inherit a trust problem none of their customers can observe directly. Verification of provider-side retention practices, not attestation by the gateway, is the control that was missing.

## GLOBAL & GEOPOLITICAL AI

**The multilateral process on autonomous weapons has reached a negative endpoint worth recording precisely: more than 120 member states of the UN Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons concluded a week-long conference in Geneva without agreeing on a regulation instrument, settling for a mandate merely to discuss one over the next two years.** The International Committee of the Red Cross and rights groups warned it will soon be too late to put the "genie back in the bottle" as development outpaces regulation (The Guardian, November 22). Dr Cordula Droege, the ICRC's director of international law, movement and partnerships, stated that AI is being fielded in the battlefield and possibly already in weapon systems, and framed the goal as a treaty of red lines rather than a total ban, with machine targeting of human beings the moral red line she named as closest to being crossed. The ICRC president and the UN secretary general have called for a legally binding instrument, including a ban on autonomous weapons used against personnel. The empirical record the coverage cites is no longer hypothetical: three civilians were killed in July by a Russian AI-guided drone that missed its target in Zaporizhzhia, and the article reports, with the attribution it carries, that a US strike on an Iranian elementary school killing 156 civilians reportedly resulted from faulty AI-gathered intelligence. Two developments sharpen the timeline pressure. Manufacturers and militaries are developing swarm capabilities where a single algorithmic error could propagate across hundreds or thousands of autonomous systems, and proliferation has already reached non-state actors, with the Houthi movement reportedly using Claude to write ballistic-missile code and groups in the Sahel reportedly using AI tools for drone ballistics and planning. The Future of Life Institute's Anna Hehir warned that further delay risks mass-casualty events that could dwarf the past two years, and that absent a treaty, private industry and militaries will set the norms themselves.

## TECHNICAL TRENDS

**A second JMLR paper this cycle documents a failure mode that practitioners of continuous-time reinforcement learning hit but rarely formalize: the discrete-time Bellman equation, the standard tool for policy evaluation, is not always a reliable approximation of the true value function because it ignores the underlying continuous-time structure.** PhiBE (Yuhua Zhu, JMLR 27(211), 2026) studies the setting where the state follows an unknown stochastic differential equation but only discrete-time data are available, and reformulates the problem as a PDE that integrates the discrete observations into a continuous-time formulation. The provable gains are specific rather than sweeping: the approach gives a more accurate value approximation especially when the underlying dynamics change slowly or the reward oscillates, with extensions to higher order for increasingly accurate approximation, and a model-free algorithm under linear function approximation whose misspecification bound has a stability factor independent of the discretization step and a sample complexity of order the inverse step, against the worse step-dependent bounds classical discrete-time LSTD analysis yields in the same setting. The most transferable contribution is the identified trade-off: finer time discretization reduces bias but amplifies variance, so excessively frequent sampling does not necessarily improve performance, an effect with no analogue in classical discrete-time analyses. For anyone building RL-based decision systems with irregularly sampled or safety-relevant feedback signals, the practical guidance is that sampling density is not free, and the MATLAB reference implementation is published for verification.
